\subsection{Target-relative evidence} 
Given an expert score $s$ with corresponding target reference scores $r_1,...,r_n$ and maximum $r_{\max}$, define $d_R=\max(r_{\max}-\textrm{median}_i r_i, 10^{-6})$, and
\begin{equation} 
E_R(s)=\begin{cases} 
-\log((1+\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}(r_i\ge s))/(n+1)), &s \le r_{\max}\\ 
\log(n+1)+\log(1+(s-r_{\max})/d_R) &s > r_{\max} 
\end{cases} 
\label{eq:evidence} 
\end{equation} 
Tied values are included in this computation. Scores exceeding the reference maximum are assigned to this score extrapolation instead of being treated as tied. Note that this latter step does not constitute a principled tail estimation procedure with any guarantees of accuracy. Each peer score yields an evidence score $e_k(x) = E_{R,k}(m_k(x))$.



\subsection{Blocks and quantiles} 
Given the number of calibration samples $M = |C|$ and the parameter $b=250$, $\lfloor M/b\rfloor$ (at least 1) contiguous blocks are constructed (i.e. block sizes will be no more than 1 apart, and preceding blocks take remaining samples if evenly divisible by $b$ is impossible -- these should thus not be interpreted as blocks of exactly 250, e.g. with 350 samples there would be 1 block of size 350). With $C_j$ denoting calibration samples in the $j^{th}$ of these $J$ blocks with size $n_j$, and the evidence scores within a block sorted to obtain $z_{j,(1)},...,z_{j,(n_j)}$, the following is defined for weight $w\in (0,1)$:
\begin{equation} 
\tau_z(w) = \max_{j = 1,..., J} z_{j, (1+\lceil(1-w)(n_j-1)\rceil)} 
\label{eq:threshold} 
\end{equation} 
corresponding to the 'higher' empirical quantile and taking the maximum over these blocks. The PACE decision criteria then simply tests whether $z(x) > \tau_z(w)$. With these (fixed) quantiles, at most a $w$ proportion of points within each respective block of calibration data exceed this quantile, i.e. no more than a $w$ block-FPR. This applies to the calibration data themselves only.

\subsection{Separately calibrated experts with joint weights scaling} 
Consider a collection of $L$ experts and desired block-FPR weight $B$. Assign each $w_{\ell}= B/L$. Then defining a scaled joint alert rule as
\begin{equation}
 U_{B,\lambda}(x)=\mathbf{1}\!\left\{\exists\ell:
 z_\ell(x)>\tau_{z_\ell}(\lambda w_\ell)\right\}.
 \label{eq:union}
\end{equation}
The calibration procedure computes this quantity within each block. It searches upward by factors of $\lambda \in [1,c]$ (by first computing at $2\lambda$ and then repeatedly doubling $\lambda$ until either the target block-wise union-FPR is exceeded or $c$ is reached; returning early if at $c$ the criterion is satisfied, and else bisection-searching for $\lambda$ between the previous feasible and current infeasible values for 20 iterations, returning at the highest feasible lower point found). The implementation sets $c=64$, and if $64 \max_{\ell} w_{\ell} \ge 1$, sets $c = 0.999/\max_{\ell} w_{\ell}$. Note that if this scaled union-FPR at $\lambda=1$ does not satisfy $B$, the procedure simply returns the corresponding initial thresholds and provides no guarantee to subsequently fix (invalid) user-specified $w_\ell$'s. For PACE, the equally split choices guarantee satisfaction of this calibration with $w_\ell$ as described by combining the above fixed-quantile argument and Bonferroni.

The returned $\lambda$ is purely the result of this numerical procedure on a discrete block-level FPR function and is not viewed as optimally representing the true block-wise FPR over some underlying population. This procedure is then run on each set of peer expert evidence scores, with $B_P$ allocated to this joint FPR of $K$ expert scores. Note that this entails $K$ separately threshold-calibrated score streams feeding the PACE decision, although with a common $\lambda$ that is informed by the \emph{joint} behaviour of these experts - hence unstable experts may exert influence on the FPRs of their co-experts through this mechanism.

\subsection{Fixed anchor score and auxiliary local scores} 
A composite average of six anomaly-detection methods is created: PCA reconstruction MSE, IF, LOF, mean k-NN distance, histograms, and shrinkage Gaussian. For each, an evidence score is calculated relative to the target reference, and the $6$ resulting evidence scores are then averaged (without transforming this average again). A PCA explaining 95\% of variance with its reconstruction MSE, an IF with 100 trees \cite{liu2008isolation}, an LOF \cite{breunig2000lof} with 20 neighbours, k-NN with an averaged distance over 10 NN (using its learned nearest neighbours structure), and a sum over the neglog smoothed empirical frequency of each sample's bin across features (for each feature with 20 bins constructed equi-distance over the training range and using an additive-one smoothing -- assigning the minimum observed training-range frequency if a test-value lies outside this range) are used. Finally, a regularised Gaussian distance (pseudoinverse with penalty $0.001$ of the target training sample covariance) gives its score (the squared Mahalanobis distance).

Additionally, 6 bank scores are constructed. Three share scores with elements of the above anchored ensemble, namely the IF, histogram and Gaussian scores. A further two use neglog featurewise scores, and the final score is an IF fit to the peer evidence scores, as follows. First, the sum of feature neglog tail scores is taken in the manner of ECOD:
\begin{equation} 
s_{tail}(x) = \sum_{q=1}^d \max\{-\log((A_q(x_q)+1)/(N+1)), -\log((N-C_q(x_q)+1)/(N+1))\}, 
\label{eq:tail} 
\end{equation} 
where $A_q(\cdot)/C_q(\cdot)$ are counting functions over target-training samples for values less-than-or-equal-to / strictly-less-than, respectively. This score is labelled `ECOD sum'. Unlike the original ECOD paper \cite{ecod2022} which takes maxima over the sum of the respective neglog L/R/tail-skew tail scores, here the elementwise max over the feature tails is instead computed and then summed, as described above and in Equation~\ref{eq:tail}. Secondly, for each randomly-chosen (with-replacement) pair of feature indices (sampled $150$ times, and excluding self-comparisons if $d > 1$ and allowing duplicates - note these therefore refer to the number sampled), categorical values are assigned to each sample based on its empirical target-training quantile membership (values are assigned to the left-side index where tied) among $1/8, ..., 7/8$-quantile cuts. After counting occurrences of each such (pair of) category values $h(x)$ and adding a Bayesian additive-half pseudo-count across all possible categories ($8+1$-levels per dimension), the neglog occurrence scores are summed over all sampled feature pairs (dividing by their number). Let $\mathcal P$ be the retained pairs and $h_{uv}(x)$ the training count in the coded cell for pair $(u,v)$.
\begin{equation}
 s_{\mathrm{pair}}(x)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal P}
 -\log\frac{h_{uv}(x)+0.5}{N+0.5\times9^2}.
 \label{eq:pair}
\end{equation}

Finally, a 100-tree isolation forest is trained on the vector $\rho(x)$ of the $K$ peer evidence scores on the target training set $x\in T_N$, and its negative forest score is used as its anomaly score. Applying the transformation of Eq~\ref{eq:evidence} with respect to scores on $R$, this final score thus models the collective action of the non-target experts on the target data. No gating decision on experts or changes to the supervised expert score calculation is performed in any case.


\subsection{Decision resolution} 
With $r=a=0.5$, an anchor and complementary local weight are assigned as
\begin{equation} 
B_A=\begin{cases} aB_L, & aB_L\ge 1/b\\ B_L,& aB_L < 1/b\end{cases}, B_B = (1-a)B_L,
\label{eq:allocation} 
\end{equation} 
calibrating each of these as a joint criterion (as described above) with the former consisting of a single expert. Denote the respective expert FPRs by $U_P, U_A$ and $U_B$, so the overall PACE decision rule is
\begin{equation} 
D(x) = U_P(x) \vee U_A(x) \vee U_B(x). 
\label{eq:decision} 
\end{equation} 
There is no subsequent global calibration of this joint decision. At nominal $\alpha=0.01$ (and with $r=a=0.5$), this corresponds to FPR weights $B_P=0.005$, $B_A=0.005$ and $B_B=0.0025$. Note that this amounts to an \emph{oversplit} of the total desired $\alpha$-budget by a further $0.0025$. Alternatively, all local scores could be jointly considered under a single $\alpha_L$ budget. Doing this at $\alpha=0.01$ gives equivalent performance to PACE to recorded precision\appendixtext{ (with near-identical MP-FPR, see Appendix~\ref{app:inventory})}. As such, the above formulation offers no clear \emph{aggregated} benefit over the single local joint FPR procedure for low nominal $\alpha$, and any divergence at target-level will need to be explored directly. Of course, similar \emph{total} observed FPRs do not rule out differing sample detections / true population-level FPR. At $\alpha = 0.05$, $B_P, B_A, B_B$ become $0.025, 0.0125, 0.0125$. In practice, these FPRs are observed to be lower than the total implied by their sum due to overlapping decision regions and rounding of attainable block-level FPRs (at most $Bn_j$ detections per block), but this alone is insufficient to justify the above weighting. The quantile resolution of each individual local score (without joint $\lambda$ adjustment) may permit $w(n_j-1)$ (strictly greater than) detections per block -- depending on block size -- so the above is simply a heuristic resolution rule ensuring that the weighted-quantile criterion is applicable (with $1/b$) to all local scores.

The protected anchor preserves a separate local decision, but does not guarantee non-inferiority against the benchmark mean-ensemble detector (consisting of 7 evidence score means including an autoencoder and using a different threshold procedure).

\subsection{Model fitting / computation requirements} 
Peer experts comprise 2-hidden layer MLPs with widths $\{64,16\}$ and ReLUs, and class-balanced two-class (attack vs. rest) logistic losses. All parameters were updated using Adam for 8 epochs at a batch size of 256 and learning rate $10^{-3}$. Local scores were then computed on $T_N$ for $N$ as defined; except 8000 points were used for the local IF and k-NN constructions. Scores (and quantiles) are then fixed at inference -- the test procedure thus performs no dynamic updating.

It is worth noting that the benchmark mean-ensemble detector likewise fits a local (target-train/test) neural net -- an autoencoder -- and includes it as one of its scores, albeit one excluded from the mean-anchor score. As such, PACE involves local net-fitting/training at execution. Inference-time complexity involves the $K$ peer classifier inferences in addition to the collection of local scores (one of which is another model trained on $K$ inputs). Each peer net requires $64d + 1138$ trainable parameters, for input size $d$. While not a direct estimate of filesize / network transfer / run-time speed / compute-energy-cost / on-device space, the above is provided solely to provide intuition over the size of the required model parameters. Further insight into these properties is outside the scope of the presented inference performance metrics.
