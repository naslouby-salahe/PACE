\section{Related work} 
\label{sec:related} 
\subsection{Calibrated supervised and benign-deviation evidence} 
Yue et al.~\cite{yue2026mcde} proposed the Mode-Calibrated Dual-Evidence Detector (MCDE). Their detector fuses a supervised histogram gradient boosting decision tree score with distance metrics to estimated benign-mode regions, maps each into empirical evidence from the benign tail, distributes weights between them, and thresholds the combined value on an independent benign dataset. In its headline setting, individual attacks and their families are omitted during evaluation. They additionally consider a device-transfer scenario, where they again re-threshold the combined evidence score on the (trusted) target's benign data, characterising the expected FPR-vs-true-positive rate.

This work is informative for PACE's treatment of combining calibrated supervised evidence with a deviation-from-benign signal. PACE differs in that it preserves a (potentially large) number of foreign supervised detectors as expert(s), alongside additional target-local terms that measure deviation from a heterogeneous local representation. It thresholds each group of evidence, namely the union over peer detectors, local anchor and bank term separately, with a low-$\alpha$ resolution step that avoids exactly re-dividing the total allocated mass. PACE also restricts peer training to the target label (rather than excluding attack families). Thus these works most closely relate in terms of the set of peer models available, the terminal evidence-thresholding, and the target knowledge available respectively. Note that MCDE is not benchmarked within the study.

Nweke~\cite{nweke2026tail} similarly constructs two tail-probability estimates over IEC~61850 traffic - one based on an empirical tail, and one estimated via the extreme-value method - which are each calculated over benign models of an autoencoder, along with a change in packet-interval. These values combine under a Fisher combination statistic which they threshold on further benign data. Notably, they do not use the corresponding analytic distribution to compute the significance of this test. This is again a useful point of reference w.r.t.~fusion over heterogeneous evidence signals. However, PACE differs in combining evidence from (foreign) supervised detectors and two locally-computed tiers (bank/anchor), again separated only at the final union step, as opposed to applying a threshold to a composite score. These methods operate over very different data and statistics, so quantitative comparisons are not expected. Some of these differences are tabulated in Table~\ref{tab:prior}.

\subsection{Empirical calibration and statistical guarantees} 
Split conformal prediction (CP) leverages a sample size correction to its threshold to guarantee finite-sample marginal CP (under exchangeability, with respect to sampling over calibration and test sets)~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal}. PACE's procedure enforces a bound on the empirical false-alert count via empirically-derived thresholds (by block and rescaled according to the number of peer detectors combined). It therefore uses a different index into the benign data, and produces an evidence estimate that should not be considered an extension to CP in this sense.

Bates et al.~\cite{bates2023outliers} define and extend CP with respect to the presence of outliers in the test set, and correct for potential correlations arising due to samples being jointly drawn in the calibration and test sets. Such refinements do not appear in PACE's procedure, which also does not concern a FDR over test instances but only controls the false alert rate w.r.t.~benign data. Further, it relies on the inputs being marginal CP (e.g., to apply the results of Vovk~\cite{vovk2020averaging} to an arbitrary correlation structure); and even in the simple case of a union-bound, such a bound relies on knowledge of the underlying probabilities of each detector exceeding its threshold over arbitrary future draws, not its empirical analogue. Simply dividing an allocated $\alpha$ uniformly, or taking the corrected tail from the observed calibration draws does not suffice.

Similarly, Barber et al.~\cite{barber2023nonexchangeable} show how weighting by past draws yields relaxed CP bounds when exchangeability is violated, but PACE does not adopt their correction method. PACE partitions the target data sequentially, takes the maximum block-wise empirical quantile, and does not henceforth guarantee any property of temporal drift. Only empiricism will determine whether the observed FPR falls below the desired limit.

Xiang et al.~\cite{xiang2026can} have recently released a related CAN method in preprint form. It distinguishes initial adaptation to target benign data from the subsequent step of empirical calibration to a limited number of temporal windows of benign traffic; choosing the number of windows based on expected serial-correlations and available sample size for CP. If correlations are unaddressed, the guarantee becomes difficult to substantiate. Here, by contrast, PACE takes the maximum quantile index obtained across temporal calibration splits and applies it to test (i.e., individual) samples; i.e., it uses different granularities of target benign data. Nonetheless, it demonstrates that adapting to target benign-only data in this sense is not unique to PACE. It does not establish guarantees for PACE's peer unions.

It is worth emphasising that the concept of selecting per-method thresholds in order to achieve a desired overall rate of alert has been considered previously in the context of collections of heterogeneous anomaly detectors~\cite{bridges2017threshold}. PACE departs from this prior body of work by jointly introducing reuse of external supervised detectors, target-relative evidence computation and thresholds, and the addition of an orthogonal benign-local component.

\subsection{Established local anomaly models} 
As standard approaches to modelling anomalous behaviour, PACE includes isolation forest and Local Outlier Factor~\cite{liu2008isolation,breunig2000lof} representing isolation-distance and distance-to-locals heuristics, respectively. Also widely applied is to learn (partially) denoising reconstruction functions and use the residuals as anomaly measures, though there are caveats around whether this necessarily encodes anomalous characteristics well if the compressed representation remains accurate~\cite{pang2021review}. The inclusion of each method in PACE builds on this literature. As there is no autoencoder included amongst the anchor models, no attempt is made to attribute the results to this effect in particular, but it is noted that the benchmark includes this model in the baseline.

\subsection{Benign-only monitoring, transfer, and personalization} 
Several existing works in network intrusion monitoring consider adapting solely to benign behaviour over the target environment. N-BaIoT learns and deploys an ensemble of autoencoder models on the device of interest, tuning detection based on further benign examples from that device~\cite{nbaiot2018}. They report performance over 9 devices subjected to attacks by Mirai and BASHLITE bots. Although comparable, there is insufficient correspondence between the unit and granularity of their decision and PACE's to directly compare results. D\"IoT considers jointly modelling a device type of traffic from the perspective of several devices, utilising a federated technique to combine parameters and avoid sending traffic between devices~\cite{diot2019}. PACE considers an external corpus of trained models (with full attack labels at the source device(s)), rather than iteratively training/aggregating in this way.

More recently, Nguyen et al.~\cite{nguyen2025fedmse} propose to combine a number of benign-fitted autoencoders and a second stage centroid-distance anomaly metric under a similar federated setting (aggregation weight determined by autoencoder MSE). Rey et al.~\cite{rey2022federated} also demonstrate federated learning over benign-only autoencoder-based detectors and classifiers, on the N-BaIoT benchmark including devices unseen during training. Abhijit et al.~\cite{hyperids2025} consider a multi-client training framework that learns parameters of a shared hypernetwork as well as an individual representation of the clients towards achieving a classification accuracy. In all cases, the learnt models / their weights are updated in this process. PACE, conversely, fixes a collection of independently-trained supervised peer models, and subsequently only updates associated thresholds (as well as using local detectors) given target benign data. Note that the benchmark involves some pre-processing combining of datasets (see Section~\ref{sec:experiment}), so does not strictly support claims of *only* exchanging target-models.

The Open Set Dandelion Network (OSDN) also leverages source labels to learn a model to deploy at an (unknown) target location~\cite{osdn2024}, using target samples which are not labelled (and may comprise known and/or novel attack traffic). PACE assumes access to trusted benign target samples as well as pre-supplied detector(s) trained to the same feature space. Thus these two scenarios can be compared for deploying to an unlabeled target environment: either leveraging all samples without knowledge of their distribution(s), and additionally learning a transformation from source to target feature spaces (OSDN), or relying on additional trusted benign-labeled information and compatible peer model(s) (PACE).

DI-NIDS also trains with source labels and transfers to target traffic, using unsupervised domain-adaptation techniques to find common invariant features from which to subsequently construct a one-class classifier~\cite{layeghy2023dinids}. Conversely, PACE's target phase uses a known collection of peer models which are compatible in their input features.

Care should be taken to distinguish from other works which employ calibration as a term to mean recalibrating a probability estimate of class-membership. For instance, Talpini et al.~\cite{talpini2025} train a number of probability-calibrated classifiers with local samples (with known class membership), again utilising a federated approach to share calibration parameters. They aim to produce well-calibrated models in the sense of probability estimation. PACE uses empirical calibration to identify an appropriate threshold over the benign tail estimate and does not seek to derive or adjust any probabilities. There is no implication from the calibrated detectors that they meet the $\alpha$ threshold on probabilities of membership in any particular class. Nor is it the case that their methods would automatically yield a detector meeting PACE's requirements.

\subsection{Fusion and detector-response representations} 
In~\cite{zimek2014}, Zimek et al.\ identify a range of desirable characteristics and potential failure cases for collections of (anomalous-score-producing) detectors. Of note, they suggest considering how to combine detectors of differing granularity/resolution, and illustrate that naively aggregating (via max.) could be adversely affected by a single erroneously high-estimating detector. PACE addresses one aspect of the latter phenomenon by individually adjusting thresholds on peer detectors in light of the target benign samples; however, the subsequent common multiplicative factor means that each detector in the union is still affected by the calibration of its peers.

There are several methods that exploit properties of multiple detectors' outputs for downstream detection. The Kitsune~\cite{kitsune2018} IDS feeds the anomaly outputs of multiple (autoencoder) detectors into a second-stage autoencoder, thus permitting interactions across individual detector reconstructions to inform anomalies. PACE analogously considers an isolation forest over a target-relative version of the multiple peer detector evidence signals. PACE's use of these specific variables as coordinates in the response-model estimator therefore differs; the practice of detecting anomalies on these meta-data does not. More recently, ViM has demonstrated the utility of augmenting anomaly signals derived from the raw feature space with a complementary measure of distance from classifier predictions in OOD detection~\cite{vim2022}; this similarity is noted but again PACE's setting is quite different (both task-domain and construction).

\subsection{Transfer evaluation and operating points} 
Jin~\cite{jin2026crossprotocol} performs a similar study to~\cite{ha2026targetfpr}, comparing window-level vs sample-level adaptation of threshold parameters for an IP-to-BLE protocol transfer problem. He observes gains in relative ordering under his target setting but poor absolute threshold performance. The latter work also transfers thresholds identified on the target's benign data (validated on further benign data) and quantifies the extent to which these can lead to high rates of false alerts (against unseen held-out attacks) for session-graphs over network hosts. Both of these studies depart from standard performance-ranking on target validation sets in their evaluation, and similarly PACE reports the actual false-positive rate observed in each experimental condition. Nouman et al.\ further identify inconsistent false positives due to a mismatch in the data granularity on which thresholds were learned (window-wise average of flow features) versus used (individual flows) in evaluation~\cite{nouman2026granularity}. PACE's threshold is learned and evaluated on the individual samples (rows), but this is a helpful indicator of possible spurious causes of the high false-positive rate observed elsewhere.
