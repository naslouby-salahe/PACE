\section{Introduction} 
\label{sec:intro} 
Freshly-deployed targets will typically have some notion of benign behaviour available for learning, but will lack labelled attacks. Concurrent peers will instead be able to contribute supervised detectors against known threats. A purely local model for attack detection would ignore peer information; using peer detections alone would fail to detect threats absent from their training sets. In this work, local and peer-based information is combined to enable local detectors to fill this gap.

A natural approach is to take the maximum peer score and calibrate against the corresponding maximum over the target benign samples to obtain a common threshold. However, if one peer consistently dominates this maximum among the target's benign samples, its higher scores may crowd out attack signals emitted from other peers at lower magnitudes. This is referred to as \emph{peer drowning}. Detector score rescaling and dominance by a strong outlier detector have been previously noted in the setting of combining anomaly scores~\cite{zimek2014}. Adjusting to target scale will not resolve peer drowning between differently-distributed detectors.

PACE (Peer-wise Calibrated Experts) uses a fixed set of peer detectors together with a local detection head based on a benign-reference anchor, and a suite of auxiliary expert detectors. Each peer's attack-minus-benign margin is transformed into target-relative evidence and separately thresholded; the peer union is calibrated with a shared multiplier. The local anchor similarly provides a rare-feature detector and detector response distribution in addition to a general-purpose anomaly detector. Detectors based on calibrated ensembling of heterogeneous base estimators have been previously explored, including by Yue et al.~\cite{yue2026mcde}. No new base estimator is proposed.

Results are reported in three parts:
\begin{enumerate} 
\item Local detector improvement, using the proposed local head versus a baseline local head (equal-weighted combination of 7 local detectors);
\item peer-wise versus combined (average/max) peer detector thresholds; and
\item additional threats detected by the local detectors beyond the peer ensemble. For which removed experts is final detection most adversely impacted?
\end{enumerate} 
The second comparison probes the effect of peer drowning, although the gains cannot be attributed strictly to it.

Using 58 detection tasks across 6 datasets, PACE detects 2.61--2.83pp more attacks at $\alpha=0.01$ and 2.32--2.34pp more at $\alpha=0.05$, target-macro average over a baseline with an equal peer head and the average of seven local detectors. Under nominal $\alpha=0.05$, both parameterisations also achieve lower average FPs across tasks. Various design decisions of the detector are ablated, and the tasks improved upon are characterised. Note that the detector was fit and validated on target data consisting only of benign samples, and evaluated on a public dataset also used during PACE development.
