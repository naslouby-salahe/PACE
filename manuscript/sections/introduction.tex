\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Monitoring a newly introduced target presents an asymmetric learning problem. The target can supply examples of legitimate operation, while other clients may already have labeled malicious traffic. A supervised detector trained only for the target is unavailable, but discarding peer attack information would leave the monitor dependent on deviations from its own benign sample. The practical question is how to preserve useful peer evidence while retaining a local route for attacks that those peers miss.

A common fusion strategy takes the largest peer score and calibrates one threshold on target benign observations. This strategy can lose discrimination when the benign tail of one peer dominates the fused score: the common threshold must accommodate that tail, and another peer's informative but numerically smaller attack evidence may no longer trigger. We call this task-specific failure mode peer drowning. Score normalization and domination by a single extreme detector in maximum fusion are established concerns in outlier ensembles~\cite{zimek2014}. Target-relative score transformations address differences in score scale, but they do not make the tails of heterogeneous experts interchangeable or ensure that a single threshold on their maximum preserves every useful detector.

PACE, Peer-wise Calibrated Experts, maps each supervised peer's margin relative to scores on a target benign reference role. Separate empirical thresholds are calibrated on a later benign role, and a shared multiplier adjusts the peer union according to its observed blockwise alert rate.

PACE addresses this setting through two complementary design choices. First, it retains the foreign supervised classifiers as separately calibrated experts rather than collapsing their scores into a single statistic before thresholding. Second, it replaces a single mean-ensemble local decision with a protected anchor and a heterogeneous bank designed to detect attacks outside the peer union. The contribution lies in how these components are combined and calibrated under the target's information restrictions, rather than in a new base anomaly estimator. Related calibrated hybrid detectors, particularly MCDE~\cite{yue2026mcde}, provide important precedent; the distinction examined here concerns peer multiplicity, the target information regime, and the structure of the final decision.

The article makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A peer-wise calibrated intrusion detector that retains separately thresholded foreign supervised classifiers and combines their union with a protected local anchor and a heterogeneous anomaly-expert bank. The implemented decision rule, reference transformation, blockwise calibration, and allocation procedure are specified precisely.
\item An evaluation of the complete local-branch replacement against a shared-peer mean-ensemble comparator across 58 targets from six corpora, with paired detection comparisons, realized false-alert measurements, and operating-point sensitivity analysis.
\item An analysis of complementary local detections, peer-fusion alternatives, expert-family ablations, corpus-level variation, and fixed-cohort fitting-budget changes, identifying where the method adds coverage and where its benefit is limited.
\end{enumerate}

Across the 58-target primary cohort, PACE improves target-macro detection over the shared-peer mean-ensemble comparator by 2.61--2.83 percentage points at nominal $\alpha=0.01$ and 2.32--2.34 points at $\alpha=0.05$. At $\alpha=0.05$, both fitting budgets also show lower mean realized false-alert rates. The largest gains occur in TON\_IoT, while the ablations identify rarity-based local evidence as an important source of complementary detections. The evaluation also characterizes target-level variability and calibration limitations rather than assuming that a nominal operating parameter guarantees a corresponding future alert rate.
