\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Monitoring a newly introduced target presents an asymmetric learning problem. The target can supply examples of legitimate operation, while other clients may already have labeled malicious traffic. A supervised detector trained only for the target is unavailable, but discarding peer attack information would leave the monitor dependent on deviations from its own benign sample. The practical question is how to preserve useful peer evidence while retaining a local route for attacks that those peers miss.

A common fusion strategy takes the largest peer score and calibrates one threshold on target benign observations. This strategy can lose discrimination when the benign tail of one peer dominates the fused score: the common threshold must accommodate that tail, and another peer's informative but numerically smaller attack evidence may no longer trigger. We call this task-specific failure mode peer drowning. Score normalization and domination by a single extreme detector in maximum fusion are established concerns in outlier ensembles~\cite{zimek2014}. Target-relative score transformations address differences in score scale, but they do not make the tails of heterogeneous experts interchangeable or ensure that a single threshold on their maximum preserves every useful detector.

PACE (Peer-wise Calibrated Experts) converts each peer's attack-minus-benign margin into target-relative upper-tail evidence, using empirical ranks on a benign reference set and extrapolation beyond its maximum. Separate empirical thresholds are calibrated on a later benign role, and a shared multiplier adjusts the peer union according to its observed blockwise alert rate.

PACE addresses this setting through two complementary design choices. First, it retains the foreign supervised classifiers as separately calibrated experts rather than collapsing their scores into a single statistic before thresholding. Second, it replaces a single mean-ensemble local decision with a protected anchor and a heterogeneous bank designed to detect attacks outside the peer union. Related calibrated hybrid detectors, particularly MCDE~\cite{yue2026mcde}, provide important precedent; the distinction examined here concerns peer multiplicity, the target information regime, and the structure of the final decision.

The contribution is the combination of frozen supervised peers, separate target-relative thresholds, and complementary local decisions. Its base estimators and empirical thresholding are established. The evaluation separates local-branch replacement from peer fusion and reports each comparison's false-alert cost. Peer drowning motivates the fusion comparison; aggregate gains alone do not identify its mechanism.

The article makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A peer-wise calibrated intrusion detector that retains separately thresholded foreign supervised classifiers and combines their union with a protected local anchor and a heterogeneous anomaly-expert bank. The implemented decision rule, reference transformation, blockwise calibration, and allocation procedure are specified precisely.
\item An architectural evaluation that separates two questions: whether the complete heterogeneous local branch improves detection over a shared-peer mean-ensemble comparator, and how separately calibrated peer decisions change detection and false alerts relative to mean and maximum peer fusion when the complete local branch is fixed. The experiments report both outcomes across 58 targets from six corpora.
\item An analysis of complementary local detections, peer-fusion alternatives, expert-family ablations, corpus-level variation, and fixed-cohort fitting-budget changes, identifying where the method adds coverage and where its benefit is limited.
\end{enumerate}

Across the 58-target primary cohort, PACE improves target-macro detection over the shared-peer mean-ensemble comparator by 2.61--2.83 percentage points at nominal $\alpha=0.01$ and 2.32--2.34 points at $\alpha=0.05$. At $\alpha=0.05$, both fitting budgets also show lower mean realized false-alert rates. The largest gains occur in TON\_IoT, and rarity-family ablations identify useful complementary evidence. The study characterizes architectural tradeoffs on a benchmark that also informed development, under benign-only target fitting and calibration.
