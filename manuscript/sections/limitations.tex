\section{Threats to validity and limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\paragraph{Evaluation scope and selection}
The evaluation covers 58 targets across six corpora; the conclusions are limited to this benchmark setting. Different learning seeds quantify variability within this cohort. This distinction between benchmark evidence and deployment claims follows established cautions about information availability, baseline coverage, and laboratory evaluation in security machine learning~\cite{arp2024pitfalls}. Eligibility requires attack support, sufficient benign calibration data, and enough peers; the resulting units do not represent all deployed devices. Resource-eligible cohorts further select for abundant retained training data. Claims apply to these cohorts and their preprocessing, rather than to arbitrary devices with the same nominal fitting budget.

\paragraph{Dependence and uncertainty}
Targets within a stratum can share peer models, data collection conditions, and attack-generation procedures. Scenario-level targets are not necessarily independent physical devices. The bootstrap samples targets within fixed corpus groups and therefore does not explicitly model all of these dependencies. Three learning seeds provide limited information about optimization variability and none about sampling new corpora. Numerous ablation and subgroup intervals are exploratory and should not be read as simultaneous confirmatory tests.

\paragraph{Information availability}
Target attack labels are excluded from the target detector, but the benchmark identifies benign traffic using dataset annotations. In deployment, obtaining a clean benign onboarding sample is itself a security assumption. Peers require labeled attacks, a compatible feature schema, and trustworthy models. The target's fitting budget excludes its extra reference and calibration roles and the complete target training contribution to pooled standardization. The study therefore does not establish a first-hours or fixed-total-sample onboarding guarantee.

\paragraph{Calibration and distribution change}
Blockwise calibration constrains empirical rates on selected benign observations. It does not establish population coverage under temporal change or cross-network transfer. The low-alpha anchor rule increases nominal component allocations, and the final OR is not recalibrated. Score extrapolation beyond reference maxima introduces an additional modeling choice. The observed false-alert distribution contradicts interpreting the nominal parameter as a uniform test-time bound. No online drift detector or recalibration policy was evaluated.

\paragraph{Baseline and mechanism attribution}
The primary comparator is an internal architectural predecessor. The campaign includes useful fusion and local-bank ablations, but not a broad set of contemporary external methods implemented under the same information constraints. MCDE is a close conceptual comparison, not an experimentally evaluated competitor. Removal ablations change union calibration as well as expert membership. The six-member anchor and seven-member mean comparator also differ in their inclusion of an autoencoder. These facts limit both novelty claims and causal explanations of specific target outcomes.

\paragraph{Benchmark and security scope}
The corpora differ in features, client definitions, attack labels, sampling, and source ordering. Pooling their target-level scores does not constitute cross-corpus model transfer. Attack families may be represented among peer development examples, so the evaluation is not uniformly a held-out-family test. Gotham\textquotesingle s complete-second aggregates can include mixed-label packets and cross a role boundary; chronological row splitting does not remove this dependence. Separately ordered client and label streams also do not establish a network-wide prospective cutoff. The study does not test adversarial peers, poisoning, evasion, encrypted-feature availability under operational collection constraints, or attacks on the monitor itself.

\paragraph{Operational cost and deployment}
The existing evidence does not measure end-to-end latency, throughput, memory, energy, communication volume, or incident-handling burden. Algebraic parameter counts cannot substitute for those measurements. The executed benchmark trains an autoencoder for its shared comparison pipeline even though the PACE anchor excludes its output. A minimal deployment implementation could have different costs, but such an implementation and its performance are outside this evaluation.
