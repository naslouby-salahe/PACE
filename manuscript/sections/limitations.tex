\section{Threats to validity and limitations} 
\label{sec:limitations} 
\paragraph{Evaluation population and dependence} 
The set of 58 targets was selected based on feasibility for our evaluation: suitable attacks and benign data to calibrate and evaluate on, as well as a large enough number of peer devices. For some of the larger training budgets, we also required a larger volume of training data to retain after filtering. The selected targets may therefore overlap substantially with each other in terms of shared peer devices, captured packet data, device/capture configuration, and attacks generated using the same base procedures. The bootstrap resampling is stratified over available corpora but does not fully account for such relatedness between targets. While we sampled multiple random training seeds, all results are evaluated on one fixed cohort that was also accessible during design, with no newly generated corpora. The results for individual subgroups / ablations are thus best interpreted individually and not to be considered jointly. More broadly, the potential issues associated with such selection and controlled 'lab-based' evaluation is a recognised issue~\cite{arp2024pitfalls}, and dataset curation is known to produce dataset-specific quirks when designing benchmarks~\cite{flood2024smells}. Such effects would need to be separately assessed for our corpus subsets of interest to evaluate whether any remain in PACE.

\paragraph{Information availability and preprocessing} 
Our corpora include annotated benign traffic assigned to specific onboarding roles, and we assume that comparable high-quality information could be obtained from actual deployments (in addition to peer attack traffic, and corresponding compatible feature sets). Our evaluation further leverages additional information beyond the benign traffic to be deployed - reference/calibration data and combined standardisation over training traffic assigned to all roles. Thus, the reported $N$ values should be understood only as a subset of the overall amount of onboarding data required. Furthermore, for Gotham we aggregate across entire second(s) of capture and hence the packet(s) used to generate any row may contain malicious packets or those otherwise not assigned to a client role; we may use 'future' packets relative to the generation of any given row; and raw packets from a given client/device may appear in more than one role. We do not therefore directly use (labeled) attack traffic as features to the deployed target device, but note the difference with this approach. Sampling per-corpus, removal of redundant entries, and corpus ordering means we can only partially reason over chronology; in particular, we do not assume (e.g.) a single temporal point at which all traffic from all clients ceases, nor a global cutoff point over all label assignments.

\paragraph{Calibration and distribution shift} 
We calibrate against observed calibration-block exceedances. As noted above, one small exception exists for low alpha budget allocations and we additionally extrapolate against reference data tails when allocating budgets. The overall OR value is then applied with no post-hoc calibration, although this leaves notable differences across individual target FPRs. We did not study any schemes to dynamically adapt the calibration data / thresholding rule as network conditions evolve, and therefore no claim is made about FPR performance against new distributions of incoming traffic.

\paragraph{Comparison and attribution} 
Our main comparison replaces the seven-detector local branch and re-calibrates against target traffic. Ablations similarly re-calibrate the remaining expert detectors. Accordingly, our experiments do not precisely delineate each model/detector contribution or demonstrate that our proposed allocation procedure is optimal. We do not directly compare against up-to-date competing systems (e.g.\ MCDE), for which we instead discuss differences in terms of applicable methodology/design choices. Finally, peer training may include attack labels also present in target evaluation, so this should not be read as a strict test of 'unseen' attacks for some of the deployed target devices. We can only transfer to new test-target devices sharing similar corpus types/schemas to the training pool.

\paragraph{Deployment considerations} 
The evaluation assumes cooperative / unmodified peer devices. The security properties of PACE against actively malicious peer nodes / attack on PACE itself (including e.g.\ tampering with peer features/data and encrypted or otherwise inaccessible feature content) has not been assessed. The approach also relies upon combining outputs from multiple models and shared combined preprocessing, so provides no (privacy-related) guarantees regarding such data exchange. Finally, no practical metrics (e.g.\ runtime/throughput, storage/comms footprint, resource consumption, response-time, handling alerts) were recorded or assessed. Our comparative baseline system requires training of its internal AE (not used by PACE), and so simple accounting of model parameters alone is insufficient to draw conclusions about any real-world implementation.
