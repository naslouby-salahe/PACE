\section{Discussion} 
\label{sec:discussion} 
\subsection{Complementary local coverage} 
Detections exclusive to the local branch only supplement those absent from the peer union. This represents up to 14.74\% (TON\_IoT) and 12.10\% (IoT-23) under the headline settings ($\alpha=0.01$, $N=400$), while being close to 0\% in N-BaIoT. This shows that higher local-only detection, in isolation, is not sufficient to deliver large improvements at the system level.

As discussed earlier, excluding the set of rules built over (pairwise/marginal) feature rarity has the greatest impact on detection rate across the relevant baseline configurations. Our findings thus indicate some benefit from exploiting the relative rarity of individual / pairs of features in addition to existing motif-/region-based anomaly detectors. We observe less pronounced degradation due to removal of the responses family. Note that we recompute the union of all remaining members after exclusion, hence this quantifies the net impact of dropping an entire family.

Finally, the baseline rule retains the local branch on top of some expert decisions. Hence under $\alpha=0.05$, the reallocation of budget to experts produces marginally better detection at the cost of more false positives. Note "protected" refers strictly to this aspect of preserving some average local decision, not as a strict guarantee against worse overall performance. We have otherwise modified the comparison rule substantially, by adding 3 experts and altering each expert's decision threshold. Thus our main contribution can be credited to wholesale substitution of a local branch, while individual removal experiments do not certify the indispensability of these elements.

\subsection{Preserving separate peer branch} 
Independent threshold calibration per expert allows a large absolute decision boundary to be associated with a given expert while allowing low absolute decisions by other experts to persist. This allows us to mitigate the hypothesised peer occlusion effect. However, peers still interact via the multiplication factor with the union of all their decision boundaries. Our results alone cannot verify whether the multiplication factor plays any critical role in these performance gains.

Section~\ref{sec:peer-fusion} compares detection against other fixed-local methods, demonstrating further lift in detection from independent peer decisions at the expense of higher average FPR. This should be distinguished from the primary finding on adopting local rules, and we defer to Section~\ref{sec:sweep-adjustment} on results from clamping that are specific to the average-local baseline, and do not permit inference on equivalent-FPR vs the peer-fused systems.

Notably, the multiplication factor leverages coincidences of decision boundaries between peers during calibration to justify relaxing the boundary, while imposing the combined decision boundary uniformly. Coincident peer detections are therefore not required for the multiplier to operate, and we cannot disentangle this effect.

\subsection{Dependence on targets and fitting sample size}
We characterise the opportunity space in terms of local detections missed by peer-based detections, but do not provide analysis to explain what features render specific datasets more amenable to local rules. Additional investigation on which targets / excluded attack instances produce these results could be informative. While it may be expected that more samples used for fitting may detect more anomalies, only the subset missed by peer rules leads to lift at the top level. Experiments on fixed cohort sizes show this is not always realised at the end-to-end metric, e.g., increased performance of the local rule despite degraded performance for PACE (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Remarks on use in practice} 
Our main results demonstrate improved detection alongside reduced FPR for $\alpha=0.05$, for both cohort size values studied. These results motivate use of the diverse local branch as part of our method in the dataset considered. Further consideration of specific target distributions of interest is warranted given our results for $\alpha=0.01$, which exhibit medians $<0.01$ FPR but mean rates $>0.03$ and high rates per target reaching $\sim 0.67$. Such behaviour in the extreme upper range limits wider adoption even with favourable means.

PACE also necessitates obtaining benign labels/traffic for the purposes of learning various components (reference set generation, calibration) in addition to pre-processing in line with Table~\ref{tab:data-access}. Our results are conditional on holding these parameters constant post-training. Future research will be required to determine appropriate criteria for refreshing references/calibration and handling the consequences of high numbers of raised alerts, as well as quantifying impacts on downstream metrics. Similarly, there will be a need to aggregate alert rows to determine relevant security incidents. Single anomalous samples will not be necessarily indicative of a relevant security issue~\cite{sommer2010closed}, and the signal-to-noise ratio of incidents depends on background rate of true positives~\cite{saito2015precision}; \cite{apruzzese2026sok} similarly highlights challenges of operationalising and summarising anomalous report generation. Our evaluation does not account for such properties.
