\section{Experimental results} 
\label{sec:results} 
\subsection{Primary detection gains and mean false-alert tradeoffs} 
For 58 targets, over three training random seeds, PACE outperforms the comparable mean-ensemble approach in terms of macro-average target detection performance for all 4 experimental setups in Table~\ref{tab:primary}. For $\alpha = 0.01$, there are improvements of 2.61 and 2.83 ppts at fitting budget sizes of 100 and 400, respectively. At $\alpha = 0.05$, the improvements are 2.34 and 2.32 ppts, respectively. Each of these has a non-zero paired bootstrap CI for the underlying corpus. Notably, PACE and this comparator share their peer subnetworks, and so compare on the effect of swapping the entire local network.

At $\alpha = 0.05$, it can be said that there is an observed aggregate benefit: improved target-macro detection alongside lower average realised false-alert rate. At 100 fitted rows, average target-detection performance rises from 87.13\% to 89.48\% and average realised FPR decreases from 5.74\% to 5.55\%. At 400 fitting rows, target detection performance goes from 87.36\% to 89.68\%, while average realised FPR goes from 6.44\% to 5.43\%. Two UNSW-G2 targets explain around 0.0080 of the drop in mean FPR by 0.0101 between the two comparators; excluding these, PACE has a reduced mean-FPR by 0.002107 (paired CI [0.000655,0.003869]). Of the 58 targets considered here, 32/23 have a decreased/increased mean FPR with PACE, and 3 see no change. So there is an increase in detection with a decrease in the average target FPR, while some targets will have increased FPR and decreased detection. Note that this comparison is at the target-macro level and with equal $\alpha$ across both approaches.

When using $\alpha = 0.01$, this tradeoff reverses to improved target-macro detection at the cost of +0.35/+0.16 ppts average FPR at budget sizes of 100/400 rows. See Section~\ref{sec:experiment} for discussion on the interpretation of the test statistic and associated bootstrap procedure at the target-macro level.

Another natural variant is to use the whole allowed size of $\alpha$ to the peer unions (and discard the local subnetwork). On average, detection scores are 0.812411 and 0.883945 for $\alpha = 0.01$ and $\alpha = 0.05$, respectively. Against these, PACE yields improved target detection by 2.10 to 2.66 ppts at $\alpha = 0.01$ and 1.08 to 1.29 at $\alpha = 0.05$, although the paired CIs do not exclude zero for these comparisons. Further, at $\alpha = 0.05$, the mean-FPR decreases by 0.010774 and 0.011890 at budget sizes of 100 and 400. While PACE therefore outperforms this baseline across the board, significance is not achieved on the detection score.

\subsection{Peer-wise calibration versus fused peer evidence} 
\label{sec:peer-fusion} 
The local networks are fixed for the PACE variants and detection performance is compared against various fusions of the peer networks. Calibrating peers independently results in 5.60 -- 7.34 ppt higher target detection than the mean at $\alpha = 0.01$ and 4.47 -- 5.25 ppts at $\alpha = 0.05$. Compared to max pooling of the peers, PACE shows higher target detection by 3.64 -- 4.56 and 3.05 -- 3.16 ppts, respectively. Again, all CIs\appendixtext{ (summarised in App.~\ref{app:inventory})} are non-zero.

This increase in detection comes at the cost of larger average FPR - varying between 3.24\% and 3.40\% ($\alpha=0.01$) and 5.43\% and 5.55\% ($\alpha = 0.05$), versus an average FPR ranging from 2.39\% to 2.61\% and 4.30\% to 4.72\% for each respective peer-network configuration. \appendixtext{Details are available in App.~\ref{app:inventory}. }These therefore suggest a target-detection / FPR trade-off between calibrating the peer networks individually and jointly (with FPR computed at the nominal FPR setting). It cannot be claimed that the improvement can be solely attributed to this phenomenon (i.e., peer-drowning) and whether this tradeoff holds at equally-realised FPR levels.

\subsection{Expert-family ablations and complementary detections} 
Upon removing the rarity-experts there is a decrease in detection by 0.86 -- 1.20 ppts and 0.60 -- 1.04 ppts for $\alpha = 0.01$ and $\alpha = 0.05$, respectively. Removing feature-specific experts and response-expert(s) leads to lower detection by 0.20 -- 0.34/0.01 -- 0.03 ppts and 0.20 -- 0.28 / 0.13 -- 0.31 ppts at $\alpha = 0.01$/$\alpha=0.05$. Thus, there is a larger decrease associated with removing the set of rarity-based experts from the expert bank, across all considered experiments\appendixtext{ (see App.~\ref{app:inventory})}.

Removing these expert subsets from the expert bank has a consequential effect on how the resulting union of local experts is calibrated. Therefore, these are not interpreted as direct contributions, nor will the sum of these improvements be considered as equivalent to the original setup. Swapping the union of expert responses to use unscaled margins with respect to peers does not alter target detection significantly, leading to changes of between 0.001101 and 0.002879, some of which fail to be significant from the paired bootstrap CI tests. While this suggests some degree of reliance on these scaled responses, it is smaller than that observed in the overall comparison against mean-ensembles above.

The choice of including an anchor in the local subnetwork affects both detection and FPR performance. In addition, this changes the fraction of budget allocated to the expert bank. For the $\alpha = 0.01$ setting, including the anchor gives better performance on the IoT-23 capture (target ID: 35-1). However, a number of targets show higher detection without the anchor included (e.g., TON\_IoT target: 192.168.1.190) - neither of which are significant when pooled. Removing the anchor for the $\alpha = 0.05$ experiments yields detection of 89.76\% and 89.84\% and corresponding average FPR of 6.05\% and 6.47\% for budget sizes of 100 and 400, respectively, with higher detection \emph{and} FPR than with the full PACE network in Table~\ref{tab:primary}. When just the local-union with a single anchor is considered, a very close detection score to PACE is similarly found at $\alpha=0.01$ (to tabular precision), with smaller changes to detection at $\alpha = 0.05$ but higher FPR.

If the local-network reserve is reduced from 0.5 to 0.25, this has a noticeable negative impact on detection (-1.94 to -2.01 ppts) with a small corresponding decrease in FPR at $\alpha = 0.01$\appendixtext{ (see Tab.~\ref{tab:all-arms}, App.~\ref{app:inventory})}. However, there is higher detection (+0.23 to +0.50 ppts) and FPR (+0.46 to +0.60 ppts) at $\alpha = 0.05$, indicating a tradeoff in the amount reserved for local-calibration.

The final PACE model considers detections that could not be detected with the union of peers and are only triggered by local detections, and detections triggered by local-OR-peer subnetwork alerts. When $N = 400$, there are target-local detections (relative to peer-undetectable targets) ranging between 0.147434 (TON\_IoT) and 0.120957 (IoT-23), with minimal values of 0.000042 on N-BaIoT for $\alpha = 0.01$. As per the final construction of PACE (Section~\ref{sec:method}), local alerts will result in additional target-detections if missed by the peer unions. These therefore offer an indicator of room for complementary target detection, but are not directly interpretable as gains against the mean-ensemble local union or a PACE variant where only local union detections are considered (and calibrated to their own target-macro false-alert rates).

\subsection{Gain concentration across targets} 
The average target gains for $\alpha=0.01/0.05$ and 100/400 target rows are largest in TON\_IoT ($0.117448 / 0.120780$ and $0.126729 / 0.104447$, respectively). With the targets in this dataset (9 in total) excluded, the pooled detection performance gains decrease to 0.009306, 0.010228, 0.005536 and 0.008299, with 95\% CIs $[0.001808, 0.019526], [0.000995, 0.024240], [0.000090, 0.014343]$ and $[0.000143, 0.022250]$, respectively. Overall, excluding TON\_IoT, there is a small positive average improvement in target detection across the primary experiments of around 0.55 to 1.02 pp, with low bounds on 3 of these CIs less than 0.001. There appear to be a small subset of driving targets for the improvement, and removing the top-3 gain-contributing targets explains ~60--89\% of this total gain depending on the comparison. Extending this exercise to remove the top-5 contributes to explain most of the remaining average target-gain, leaving pooled improvements of ~0.11 - 0.63 ppts. Caution must be taken with this latter analysis given that it was determined with knowledge of these outcomes.

There are minimal improvements seen for targets in N-BaIoT ($\leq 10^{-5}$ on average) given their high existing detection scores. There are modest improvements for targets in the Gotham network (0.001950 -- 0.005907) and mixed improvements for UNSW G2 targets at the different nominal $\alpha$s (+0.009251/+0.005548 and -0.001420/-0.002037, respectively). Many datasets have overlapping CIs that include zero across targets, and so average improvements on a target-corpus basis should be interpreted cautiously in this context. Indeed, the majority of targets experience an average improvement of 0 after averaging over seeds, and so gains in the overall pool arise from a subset of targets experiencing stronger improvements.

Target-specific detection differences are similarly pronounced within IoT-23 (primary mean-improvements between 0.017582 -- 0.029670). Taking the comparison for $\alpha = 0.01, N = 400$, CTU-13 sees average improvement of 0.003156 (-0.007871, 0.017066) - with its only regression to scenario 12 (loss of 0.032595; all other targets having decreases $\leq 0.01$; all decreases across the primary comparison being < 0.05). After averaging across random seeds, CTU-13 sees its bulk decrease on seed 7 at $\alpha = 0.01$ due to fewer detections made by the peer-subnetwork (only detecting ~0.189 of evaluated attacks) than in the local-union comparator. Conversely, PACE has greater detection performance on the same target at $N=100$ for the same seed. So the target-mean decrease can be attributed to this specific budget-seed instance, but not to a general phenomenon with PACE. Overall, using an absolute target-mean detection change threshold of 10$^{-3}$ for this example, there are 21 improved, 36 negligible-changed, and one regressing target at $\alpha = 0.01$, and 2 regressions (with 15 / 41 improvements / negligible at $\alpha = 0.05$). However, given that the targets share peers, these improvements should not be generalised outside this benchmark.

\subsection{False-alert heterogeneity} 
Table~\ref{tab:fpr} summarises per-target false-alert rates of PACE, mean-over-learning-seeds. PACE's mean FPR is 0.033992 ($N=100$) and 0.032369 ($N=400$), whereas the target-medians are 0.006485 and 0.006419, implying marked dispersion. The single worst target is IoT-23 capture 7-1 with mean FPR of 0.669005 and 0.670842 respectively. Around 26\% of targets exceed the specified level two-fold at $\alpha=0.01$. Consequently, the chosen value of $\alpha = 0.01$ is not indicative of a per-test 1\% risk of alerting.

There is analogous dispersion of false alerts for the comparator: its mean is 0.030516 and 0.030781 with a maximum target false-alert rate of 0.670174 and 0.670842. It is therefore concluded that training on preceding benign examples does not ensure that the type I error will be uniformly bounded by $\alpha$ in subsequent benign periods. The data can only say that these behaviours were observed, and cannot attribute the behaviour to drift, data composition, and/or poor generalisation of learned scores. It is additionally noted that the exception for the low-$\alpha$ allocations in Sec~\ref{sec:method} was in force, regardless of any shift to the test period's data generating mechanism. While informative of the possibility that such behaviour can arise from PACE, it does not rule it out entirely: for example, IoT-23 capture 34-1 suffers 11.52\%/25.54\% mean FPR under $\alpha=0.01$ and $N=100$ for the comparator/PACE, respectively.

Various read-only investigations point towards a connection with false alerts above the expected and a variation of characteristics of the benign periods. Specifically, IoT-23 capture 7-1 (calibration period has 1,996 benign examples) shows that $\sim$66\% of test period benign examples lie outside of the calibration values of \texttt{orig\_ip\_bytes}. The correlation between the magnitude of FPR increase with lower numbers of benign examples is clear (across the 58 target-means for $\alpha = 0.01$, $N=100$), although it is noted that this comparison mixes corpus sizes with $N$. Such investigations provide potential insights and further lines of enquiry but do not conclusively assign blame to a detector component nor establish that increasing $N$ would alleviate this issue.

The mean for PACE is 0.055453/0.054337 at $\alpha=0.05$ with a 90th percentile target FPR of 0.162115/0.159692. Once again, the maximum realised value is high (0.671844). There is hence poor control of the high quantiles of the FPR distribution, notwithstanding that the overall (mean) rate is close to $\alpha$. Note that the false alert probability of an individual row should not be conflated with the expected volume of alerts to be triaged in a deployment setting (e.g. dependent on the number of rows aggregated into a single alert, repeat alerts being discarded, capacity limits of operators, etc. ).

\subsection{Operating-point adjustment and common support} 
\label{sec:sweep-adjustment} 
By matching operating points against PACE's fixed sweep of 4 points, mean comparator-local adjusted gains of 2.47pp / 2.57pp / 2.22pp / 2.07pp are obtained in the primary scenarios. Paired confidence intervals are [0.009682, 0.042034], [0.009690, 0.044983], [0.005135, 0.042086] and [0.002056, 0.042482] (\emph{proportions}). For each seed, the sweep results are ordered by achieved FPR over the benign period and evaluated at the comparator's achieved FPR (if achievable in the range; if not, evaluated at the nearest extreme), before means are taken over seeds and targets. All primary results reported here are therefore an approximation to an adjustment for equal-FPR comparisons, and may be overestimated where PACE is forced to operate outside the swept values.

Table~\ref{tab:support} details where the comparison was possible. Across target$\times$seeds, between 57 and 84\% were in-sweep for $\alpha=0.01$/$0.05$ respectively. Evaluation also covers removing target$\times$seed pairs without comparator--PACE common support (i.e.\ requiring the comparison is evaluable in \emph{all} 3 learning seeds); averages continue to be taken across learning seeds as before and thus equal weights are placed on targets when calculating scenario gains. Here, PACE retains positive gains except at $\alpha=0.01$ and $N=400$ where it is negligibly small. Of course, conditioning on the achieved values here forms study-dependent subsets, which precludes using these estimates to infer the primary scenarios or the removed targets.

For completeness, it is noted that across the 174 total target-seed combinations, there are 72/59/35/41 exact matches with sweep FPR in the primary settings; other covered cells are interpolated and uncovered cells use an endpoint. There is no guarantee that an identical empirical FPR value implies a corresponding equal population-FPR; indeed, 3/6/1/2 target-seed comparisons achieve identical FPR to a sweep point but with different detector values (the original tie-break from the reporting code is maintained rather than adopting the best-case detector). Note therefore that while this constitutes another (more conservative) estimate of PACE's advantage in the primary scenarios, a formal evaluation of PACE at alternative thresholds is not being made. Where the comparator is evaluated outside the swept region, PACE's detected/undetected outcome is adopted at either the maximum/minimum sweep point as appropriate; i.e.  a claim of equal-FPR cannot be made at those points. Similarly, as matching is by \emph{test}-period achieved FPR, this adjustment could not be made ex-ante to determine an optimal onboarding threshold. It is reiterated that the separate calculation of the \textsc{Max} fusion (based on the monotone sweep of thresholds, but not using interpolation/extrapolation beyond the available points) is distinct from this. Neither calculation addresses comparisons against the two additional peer-fusion variants using the fixed local branches.

\subsection{Effect of local fit size} 
Across the primary scenarios on all 58 targets, PACE improves by $0.005582$ CI[$-0.003760$, $0.015387$] / $0.002070$ CI[$-0.004456$, $0.008690$] with a larger budget of $N$ for the local detector ($N=100\to 400$) at $\alpha = 0.01 / 0.05$ respectively. While the local detector may gain coverage as $N$ increases, the overlap with the peer detectors limits the potential of the composite detector to scale comparably.

For the universal cohort at $N=750$, there are gains vs.  the comparator of 0.024276/0.022614, however at $\alpha=0.05$ the gain excluding TON\_IoT is compatible with zero. Moving to $N=2000$, the cohort shrinks to the 47 targets with sufficient examples in their respective training periods; the gains over the comparator reduce to 0.019624 / 0.007358 and are compatible with zero by operating-point matching. At $\alpha=0.05$, without TON\_IoT, the gain becomes $-0.000370$ CI[$-0.000712$, $-0.000074$].

The final cohort comprises 15 targets and is able to accommodate up to 20k local examples. Across $\alpha$ settings there are nominal gains of 0.005329 / $-0.001311$, again with compatible CIs. Interestingly, expanding $N$ from the smallest value of 100 to 20k at $\alpha=0.05$ reduces performance by 0.028296 CI[$-0.032827$, $-0.023766$]. Most of this reduction is attributable to TON\_IoT host 192.168.1.190 which decreases by $-0.362268$. When removing this host, the remaining 14 targets have a change of $-0.004441$ with a 95\% CI of [$-0.009296$, $0.000412$]. Since the corpus-stratified CIs fix the TON\_IoT effect, given that it only contains one target for this scenario, these results do not suggest that expanding $N$ systematically harms performance of the local detector. On the other hand, they also do not suggest any clear scaling benefit of increasing local resources. Finally, no attempt is made to pool results across the varying cohort definitions to compare detector performance at differing $N$s, since the cohort sizes differ and confound with the underlying targets/corpora.

\subsection{Learning-seed robustness and acceptance testing} 
All primary scenarios have strictly positive pooled gains under every individual learning seed (min/max = 0.015977/0.039551). Clearly this is weaker evidence than demonstrating improved detection under new unseen targets but is consistent across the choice of initial random weights. When excluding TON\_IoT, learning seed 6 at $\alpha=0.05$, $N=400$ yields a (small) negative gain of $-0.000195$. Again, all pooled leave-1-study-stratum-out gains are positive, though on the same suite of studies/targets.

The results pass the repo-defined engineering criteria. This allows a maximum proportion of degrading targets, and constrains the increase in target-level realised FPR and aggregate improvements to be positive, but is not equivalent to any statistical validation of non-degradation. In particular, scenario-12 is identified as regressing under the repo criterion although achieves the global pass-condition. It is also noted that some results under expanded budgets do not meet the individual per-target criteria for degrading targets / FPR increases, hence it is not proposed to accept those scenarios based on the analysis.
