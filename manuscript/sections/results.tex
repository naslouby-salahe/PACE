\section{Experimental results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Primary detection gains and mean false-alert tradeoffs}
Across 58 targets and three learning seeds, PACE improves target-macro detection relative to the mean-ensemble local comparator in all four primary configurations (Table~\ref{tab:primary}). At nominal $\alpha=0.01$, the gains are 2.61 and 2.83 percentage points with 100 and 400 fitting rows. At $\alpha=0.05$, the corresponding gains are 2.34 and 2.32 points. All four corpus-stratified paired bootstrap intervals exclude zero. Because the two methods share the same peer branch, these comparisons measure the benefit of replacing the complete local branch.

The $\alpha=0.05$ configurations provide the clearest aggregate benefit: detection improves while mean realized false-alert rates decrease. At 100 rows, detection rises from 87.13\% to 89.48\%, while mean FPR falls from 5.74\% to 5.55\%. At 400 rows, detection rises from 87.36\% to 89.68\%, while mean FPR falls from 6.44\% to 5.43\%. At $\alpha=0.05$ and $N=400$, two UNSW G2 targets account for approximately 0.0080 of the 0.0101 mean-FPR reduction. Excluding these targets, PACE still reduces mean FPR by 0.002107, with a paired interval of $[0.000655,0.003869]$ for the reduction magnitude. Across all 58 targets, 32 have lower FPR under PACE, 23 have higher FPR, and three are unchanged. At the aggregate level, higher detection therefore accompanies lower mean FPR; individual targets need not improve on both measures. This comparison concerns target-macro outcomes at the same nominal parameter.

At nominal $\alpha=0.01$, detection also improves, although mean FPR increases by 0.003476 and 0.001588 at the two budgets. These gains therefore accompany an alert cost. The conditional interpretation of the paired intervals and the target-macro estimator follow Section~\ref{sec:experiment}.

A simpler alternative allocates the entire nominal operating parameter to the supervised peer union and removes the local branch. This peers-only rule achieves detection rates of 0.812411 at $\alpha=0.01$ and 0.883945 at $\alpha=0.05$. PACE improves on these means by 2.10--2.66 and 1.08--1.29 percentage points, respectively, although the paired detection intervals include zero. At $\alpha=0.05$, PACE also reduces mean realized FPR relative to peers-only by 0.010774 and 0.011890 at fitting budgets of 100 and 400 rows. These comparisons complement the primary local-branch replacement experiment without establishing statistically resolved detection superiority over the peers-only rule.

\subsection{Peer-wise calibration versus fused peer evidence}
\label{sec:peer-fusion}
The peer-fusion arms keep PACE's entire local branch, including its anchor and expert bank, fixed. PACE exceeds mean-fused peer detection by 0.073437 and 0.055978 at $\alpha=0.01$ and by 0.052459 and 0.044723 at $\alpha=0.05$, for $N=100$ and $400$, respectively. Against maximum-fused peers, the corresponding differences are 0.045555, 0.036390, 0.030484, and 0.031644. All eight paired descriptive detection intervals exclude zero (\ref{app:inventory}).

These nominal detection advantages accompany higher mean realized FPR. Mean-fused peers yield FPRs of 0.026093 and 0.024896 at $\alpha=0.01$, and 0.047157 and 0.044361 at $\alpha=0.05$. Maximum-fused peers yield 0.025120, 0.023855, 0.046431, and 0.043049 in the same order, compared with PACE's 0.033992, 0.032369, 0.055453, and 0.054337. The comparisons quantify the additional detection and false-alert cost of retaining separate peer decisions. They establish an operating tradeoff at the tested nominal settings; exact equal-FPR superiority and peer drowning as the causal mechanism remain unresolved.

\subsection{Which components supply complementary detections?}
Removing the rarity family costs 0.008626 and 0.011977 in pooled detection at $\alpha=0.01$, compared with 0.003413 and 0.002013 for removing feature experts, and 0.002008 and 0.002796 for removing the response expert. At $\alpha=0.05$, the rarity costs remain 0.006020 and 0.010403; feature costs are only 0.000127 and 0.000344. The response expert's costs are 0.003118 and 0.001269. Rarity-based evidence thus makes the largest measured contribution among the removed expert families in all four primary configurations.

Ablation changes also recalibrate the remaining union. They are not additive decompositions of the gain and cannot be summed to assign causal importance. Replacing target-relative peer responses by raw margins changes pooled detection by only 0.001101--0.002879 across primary settings. Several corresponding intervals include zero. The evidence for the particular response representation is therefore weaker than the overall comparison with the mean-ensemble local branch.

The anchor has a tradeoff. Removing it also reallocates the entire local budget to the expert bank; the ablation therefore changes both the decision structure and the bank allocation. At $\alpha=0.01$, the anchor-containing rule benefits IoT-23 capture 35-1, while other targets, including TON\_IoT host 192.168.1.190, favor the bank-only alternative. The pooled intervals include zero. At $\alpha=0.05$, the bank without the anchor detects more: its rates are 0.897598 and 0.898372, versus PACE's 0.894767 and 0.896837. Its false-alert rates are also higher, at 0.060458 and 0.064650. A single local union gives identical detection to PACE at $\alpha=0.01$ to the precision of the stored table; at $\alpha=0.05$ its differences are small and its false-alert rates higher. These outcomes characterize an architectural and allocation tradeoff rather than an isolated causal effect of the anchor, with the anchor-free bank favoring detection at the larger nominal parameter.

Within the final PACE rule, the attack-detection decomposition distinguishes alerts unique to the local branch from detections shared with peers. At $\alpha=0.01$ and 400 rows, local-exclusive detection is 0.147434 in TON\_IoT and 0.120957 in IoT-23, but only 0.000042 in N-BaIoT. Under the final OR rule, a local alert adds detection only when peers miss the attack. These local-exclusive coverage fractions identify the opportunity for complementary detection; they are not gains over the mean-ensemble comparator or results for a separately calibrated local-only detector.

\subsection{Where are the gains concentrated?}
TON\_IoT has the largest stratum mean gains: 0.117448 and 0.126729 at $\alpha=0.01$, and 0.120780 and 0.104447 at $\alpha=0.05$, for 100 and 400 fitting rows respectively. Removing its nine targets reduces the pooled gains to 0.009306, 0.010228, 0.005536, and 0.008299. Their intervals are $[0.001808,0.019526]$, $[0.000995,0.024240]$, $[0.000090,0.014343]$, and $[0.000143,0.022250]$. Excluding TON\_IoT, the four primary pooled gains remain positive, ranging from 0.55 to 1.02 percentage points. Three of the four corresponding interval lower bounds are below 0.001. The three largest contributing targets account for approximately 60--89\% of the summed gains, depending on the configuration. As an exploratory sensitivity analysis, removing the five largest-gain targets still leaves positive pooled gains of approximately 0.11--0.63 percentage points. These removals are selected by observed outcomes.

N-BaIoT is already near complete detection and changes by approximately $10^{-5}$ or less. Gotham gains range from 0.001950 to 0.005907. UNSW G2 improves by 0.009251 and 0.005548 at $\alpha=0.01$, but changes by $-0.001420$ and $-0.002037$ at $\alpha=0.05$. Several stratum intervals include zero. The median target gain is zero in many strata, so a positive mean should not be paraphrased as an improvement for most devices.

IoT-23's primary mean gains range from 0.017582 to 0.029670, but its target-level pattern is concentrated rather than uniform. At $\alpha=0.01$ and 400 rows, CTU-13's mean gain is 0.003156 with interval $[-0.007871,0.017066]$. Scenario 12 loses 0.032595, the only primary target loss exceeding 0.01. No primary target loses more than 0.05. Most of the seed-averaged loss for CTU-13 scenario 12 at $\alpha=0.01$ and $N=400$ occurs in seed 7, where the peer union detects only 0.189 of evaluated attacks and the comparator's local route recovers more detections. At $N=100$, the same seed favors PACE. This localizes the regression to a particular seed and fitting-budget configuration without identifying a detector-level cause.

Using a descriptive tolerance of 0.001, at $\alpha=0.01$ and $N=400$, PACE improves 21 targets, is effectively unchanged on 36, and regresses on one. At $\alpha=0.05$ and the same budget, the corresponding counts are 15, 41, and two. The targets share peer models, so these counts describe the observed benchmark rather than independent deployment outcomes.

\subsection{Are false alerts compatible with the nominal parameter?}
Table~\ref{tab:fpr} gives the distribution of target false-alert rates after averaging over learning seeds. At $\alpha=0.01$, PACE's mean is 0.033992 at 100 rows and 0.032369 at 400 rows, while the medians are 0.006485 and 0.006419. The large difference between means and medians indicates substantial heterogeneity. The worst target averages are 0.669005 and 0.670842. About 26\% of targets exceed twice the nominal parameter in both settings. A nominal value of 0.01 therefore cannot be interpreted as a one-percent test-time false-alert bound.

The comparator has the same qualitative failure: at $\alpha=0.01$, its mean rates are 0.030516 and 0.030781, and its worst-target rates are 0.670174 and 0.670842. Calibration on earlier benign data therefore does not deliver uniform nominal control on the later benign role. The recorded outcomes do not isolate temporal change, sample selection, or score extrapolation as its cause. The low-alpha allocation exception in Section~\ref{sec:method} also applies before any test-distribution change.

Read-only diagnostics reveal associations between excessive false alerts and changes in benign traffic across data roles. In IoT-23 capture 7-1, approximately 66\% of benign test observations fall outside the calibration range of the \texttt{orig\_ip\_bytes} feature. Higher false-alert rates are also associated with smaller calibration sets across the benchmark, although corpus and sample-size effects are confounded. These observations identify useful diagnostic directions without isolating a responsible detector or proving that a larger calibration set would correct the problem.

At $\alpha=0.05$, PACE's means are 0.055453 and 0.054337; the corresponding 90th percentiles are 0.162115 and 0.159692. The worst value remains 0.671844. Even where the pooled average approaches the nominal parameter, the upper tail is unsuitable evidence for uniform operational control. A row-level false-alert rate is also not an incident-level alert burden: aggregation, duplicate suppression, analyst capacity, and the benign-to-attack prevalence of a deployed stream were not measured.

\subsection{How much remains after operating-point adjustment?}
\label{sec:sweep-adjustment}
The stored budget sweep yields primary comparator-adjusted gains of 0.024659, 0.025735, 0.022176, and 0.020718 for the four settings in Table~\ref{tab:primary}. The four intervals, in the same order, are $[0.009682,0.042034]$, $[0.009690,0.044983]$, $[0.005135,0.042086]$, and $[0.002056,0.042482]$. These estimates remain positive, but their interpretation requires the actual interpolation procedure.

For each target and learning seed, the reporting code sorts PACE's four sweep points by realized benign false-alert rate and linearly interpolates detection at the comparator's rate. When that rate falls outside the observed sweep, the calculation uses the nearest endpoint. It then averages the resulting differences over seeds and targets. Consequently, these figures are clamped sweep-adjusted comparisons, not evidence that every detector was evaluated at precisely the same false-alert rate. The primary calculation also differs from a separate max-fusion reporting routine that constructs a monotone envelope and rejects uncovered operating points. Claims about primary matched comparisons must use the former definition. The narrow four-point sweep and endpoint handling limit how much this analysis can resolve operating-point confounding. Of 174 target--seed cells per setting, the comparator rate falls below or above the PACE sweep in 47/28 cells at $\alpha=0.01$, $N=100$, and 49/20 cells at $N=400$. At $\alpha=0.05$, these counts are 12/24 and 9/19. Thus 75, 69, 36, and 28 comparisons respectively use endpoints outside the covered range. These counts are derived from the stored campaign sweeps; they make explicit how often the reported adjustment cannot actually match the comparator rate.

When the comparator's realized FPR falls below the evaluated PACE sweep, endpoint clamping uses PACE detection measured at a higher FPR. When the comparator's rate exceeds the sweep, clamping uses detection measured at a lower FPR. Consequently, these uncovered operating points do not identify the detection difference at exactly equal FPR. The sweep-adjusted results are an approximate sensitivity analysis against the mean-local comparator. Because the adjustment uses realized test FPR, it is a retrospective comparison, not a threshold-selection procedure available during benign-only fitting and calibration. It does not establish matched-FPR superiority over the fixed-local-branch peer-fusion arms.

\subsection{Does more local fitting data reliably help?}
On the same 58 targets, increasing the fitting budget from 100 to 400 rows changes PACE detection by 0.005582 at $\alpha=0.01$, with interval $[-0.003760,0.015387]$, and by 0.002070 at $\alpha=0.05$, with interval $[-0.004456,0.008690]$. Neither interval excludes zero. The local branch alone gains much more coverage, but much of that coverage overlaps detections already supplied by peers. The combined rule need not improve proportionally to the local branch.

At 750 rows, the universal cohort retains gains over the comparator of 0.024276 and 0.022614. However, at $\alpha=0.05$, the interval for the gain excluding TON\_IoT includes zero. The 2000-row analysis contains only the 47 targets with enough training-role observations. Its gains over the comparator are 0.019624 and 0.007358; both sweep-adjusted intervals include zero. At $\alpha=0.05$, the gain excluding TON\_IoT is negative, $-0.000370$, with interval $[-0.000712,-0.000074]$.

The 20,000-row cohort contains 15 targets. Its nominal gains are 0.005329 at $\alpha=0.01$ and $-0.001311$ at $\alpha=0.05$, with intervals including zero. Within this cohort, increasing the budget from 100 to 20,000 rows at $\alpha=0.05$ reduces PACE detection by 0.028296, with interval $[-0.032827,-0.023766]$. In the 15-target high-resource cohort, this decrease is concentrated in TON\_IoT host 192.168.1.190, whose detection changes by $-0.362268$. Excluding this host, the remaining 14 targets have a mean change of $-0.004441$, with a paired interval of $[-0.009296,0.000412]$. Because TON\_IoT contributes only one target to this cohort, the corpus-stratified bootstrap holds its contribution fixed. The narrow pooled interval therefore describes the observed cohort under this resampling procedure and does not establish a general decrease in detection from using more local fitting data. The results do not support monotonic improvement with additional local fitting data. Comparisons across the 58-, 47-, and 15-target cohorts would confound fitting budget with target composition and are not used as estimates of budget effects.

\subsection{Seed stability and engineering acceptance}
Every learning seed has a positive primary pooled gain, ranging from 0.015977 to 0.039551 across settings. This consistency is narrower than generalization to new targets: all three seeds reuse the same data and splits. Excluding TON\_IoT, seed 6 has a gain of $-0.000195$ at $\alpha=0.05$ and 400 rows. Every primary leave-one-study-stratum-out pooled gain is positive, but this sensitivity check still operates on the same collection of corpora and targets.

The primary cells satisfy the repository's engineering acceptance criteria, which permit a limited fraction of harmed targets, bound changes in realized false alerts, and require positive aggregate summaries. This is not a formal statistical non-inferiority test or independent external validation. In particular, the observed scenario-12 regression remains a regression despite satisfying the aggregate acceptance rule. The larger-budget non-gating analyses contain failures of individual criteria and are reported as such, rather than being used to extend the primary verdict.
